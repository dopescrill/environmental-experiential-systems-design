# 1. Program charter

## 1.1 Purpose

This curriculum is a structured, one-year professional development
program for building a credible direction in **Environmental &
Experiential Systems Design**. It combines the user's existing education
and experience with targeted technical learning, applied projects, and
portfolio development.

The aim is not to collect certificates or learn software in isolation.
The aim is to develop an integrated capability: understand how a place
functions as a whole, identify relationships and constraints, gather and
evaluate evidence, design interventions, and use spatial, digital,
operational, and human-centered tools to improve outcomes.

## 1.2 Professional positioning

**Working professional identity:** Environmental & Experiential Systems
Designer.

**Positioning statement:**

> I design systems that connect landscapes, environmental processes,
> people, operations, and technology to improve how places function and
> evolve over time.

A related plain-language explanation:

> I'm interested in designing how places function, not just how spaces
> look or how apps work. I bring together landscape architecture,
> human-centered design, systems thinking, environmental stewardship,
> and technology.

This is a positioning statement, not a claim that every employer uses
this exact job title. Search for adjacent roles and responsibilities as
well as the exact phrase.

## 1.3 Existing strengths to leverage

-   Master's degree in Landscape Architecture.
-   Approximately two years of professional landscape architecture
    practice.
-   15+ years of UX, web, and product design experience.
-   Approximately 10 years of full-time freelance digital/web contract
    work.
-   Practical landscaping and manual-work experience.
-   Experience thinking about information architecture, user journeys,
    workflows, interfaces, and digital products.
-   Interest in ecology, land stewardship, spatial environments, outdoor
    recreation, hospitality, and the relationships between physical and
    digital experiences.
-   Existing QGIS installation and initial sample-data project using
    Alaska data and NAD27 source/destination CRS work.
-   MacBook Pro as the primary computer.
-   Claude Code as a planned AI-assisted development tool.

The curriculum should build on these strengths. Do not spend substantial
time repeating beginner UX training. Prioritize GIS, environmental
evidence, systems engineering, data/software foundations, and integrated
applied work.

## 1.4 Program outcomes

By the end of the program, aim to be able to:

1.  Explain and diagram a complex outdoor property or destination as an
    interconnected socio-ecological and operational system.
2.  Acquire, inspect, clean, document, transform, and analyze geospatial
    data in QGIS.
3.  Explain coordinate reference systems, datums, projections,
    transformations, spatial accuracy, and data limitations.
4.  Produce a coherent environmental and property spatial atlas using
    defensible data sources.
5.  Assess environmental conditions and risks using appropriate
    indicators, field methods, public datasets, and uncertainty
    statements.
6.  Define system boundaries, stakeholders, requirements, workflows,
    dependencies, feedback loops, risks, and performance measures.
7.  Design human-centered guest and employee experiences that account
    for physical space, service delivery, accessibility, environmental
    context, and supporting technology.
8.  Use Python for repeatable data processing and analysis; use SQL and
    a relational database to structure and query observations and
    assets.
9.  Understand APIs, JSON, basic automation, and responsible AI-assisted
    workflows.
10. Build at least one integrated prototype or demonstrator connecting
    spatial information, structured observations, analysis, and a human
    decision workflow.
11. Produce a portfolio of credible case studies that clearly
    distinguish actual work, public-data analysis, hypothetical
    scenarios, and proposed interventions.
12. Translate the work into job-search language, a targeted resume,
    portfolio, and interview examples.

## 1.5 Scope and boundaries

**Included** - GIS and spatial intelligence. - Systems engineering and
systems thinking. - Environmental management and ecological
stewardship. - Service, guest, employee, and experience design. - Data,
software, automation, and applied AI. - Real-world observation and
learning where permitted. - Portfolio development and job-market
preparation.

**Not the goal** - Becoming a licensed professional in every adjacent
discipline within one year. - Replacing a qualified ecologist,
hydrologist, civil engineer, surveyor, arborist, agronomist, or GIS
professional. - Claiming professional competency based solely on online
course completion. - Building an unnecessarily complex software platform
before validating a real use case. - Collecting expensive certifications
without a clear job-market reason. - Publishing confidential employer,
employee, guest, member, property-security, or operational information.

## 1.6 Evidence labels

Every substantial project artifact must be labeled with one of these
evidence types:

-   **FIELD-OBSERVED:** Personally observed or recorded in the field,
    with date, method, and context.
-   **SOURCE-DERIVED:** Taken from a cited public, licensed, or approved
    dataset/document.
-   **ANALYZED:** Produced through a documented analytical method using
    identified inputs.
-   **HYPOTHESIS:** A proposition that has not yet been verified.
-   **CONCEPTUAL:** A proposed design or model, not implemented or
    tested.
-   **PROTOTYPED:** Built and tested in a limited setting; scope and
    test method stated.
-   **IMPLEMENTED:** Put into actual operation with permission; describe
    evidence of use and outcomes.
-   **UNKNOWN / TO VALIDATE:** A material gap requiring further
    research.

Never present a hypothesis or conceptual proposal as a measured fact or
implemented improvement.

## 2.2 ADHD-friendly execution rules

-   Keep only **one primary course or learning track active at a time**.
-   Every session begins with one visible next action, not a vague goal.
-   Use 25-minute focus blocks with 5-minute breaks; take a longer break
    after several cycles.
-   End each session by recording what changed and the next action.
-   Produce a tangible artifact whenever practical: a map, diagram,
    query, short script, table, field record, or written analysis.
-   Use a "minimum viable session" of 15--20 minutes when energy is low.
-   Do not repay missed sessions by sacrificing rest or stacking extra
    hours.
-   Review progress weekly; adjust the plan monthly.
-   Keep Monday and Friday free unless there is a deliberate, temporary
    reason to move a session.

# 4. Learning area 1 --- GIS and spatial intelligence

## 4.1 Why this matters

GIS is the spatial intelligence foundation. It allows environmental,
physical, operational, and human-use information to be related
geographically. It connects directly to landscape architecture while
adding analytical and data capabilities.

## 4.2 Core competencies

-   Spatial data formats: vector, raster, GeoPackage, GeoJSON,
    shapefile, CSV with coordinates, DEM.
-   Coordinate reference systems (CRS), geographic vs projected
    coordinates, datums, map projections, coordinate transformations.
-   Attribute tables, joins, filters, field calculations, geometry
    operations.
-   Raster interpretation, elevation models, hillshade, contours, slope,
    aspect.
-   Hydrology and watershed concepts; flow direction and accumulation
    where data supports them.
-   Spatial overlays, buffers, proximity, clipping, intersections, area
    and distance calculations.
-   Cartographic hierarchy, scale, legends, labels, symbology, layout,
    metadata.
-   Data quality, positional accuracy, resolution, temporal currency,
    lineage, licensing.
-   Remote sensing and imagery interpretation.
-   Field data collection and integration.
-   Introductory geospatial Python and eventually PostGIS.

## 4.3 QGIS starting point: existing Alaska sample-data project

**Starting status:** QGIS is installed. An initial sample project has
been started using Alaska data, with NAD27 source/destination CRS work
already underway.

Do not discard or casually overwrite this project. First create a copy
or versioned working copy and document its current state.

### Initial baseline checklist

-   [ ] Record QGIS version and macOS version.
-   [ ] Record the sample-data source, download location, date, and
    license/usage terms.
-   [ ] Record the current QGIS project CRS.
-   [ ] List each layer and its declared source CRS.
-   [ ] Distinguish a layer's assigned CRS from a genuinely
    transformed/reprojected dataset.
-   [ ] Record any warning messages or datum-transformation prompts.
-   [ ] Save a baseline copy of the project before changing layer or
    project settings.
-   [ ] Create a short `README.md` describing what the project is
    intended to teach.
-   [ ] Keep a CRS investigation log with screenshots or notes where
    useful.

### CRS and datum learning checklist

-   [ ] Explain the difference between a coordinate reference system and
    a coordinate operation.
-   [ ] Explain geographic CRS vs projected CRS.
-   [ ] Explain what a datum represents and why NAD27 and modern datums
    may not align exactly.
-   [ ] Identify the source CRS from authoritative metadata, not by
    guessing from the location.
-   [ ] Identify the intended destination CRS based on the
    mapping/analysis purpose and geographic extent.
-   [ ] Understand that setting the project CRS does not necessarily
    reproject the source data on disk.
-   [ ] Understand on-the-fly display reprojection in QGIS.
-   [ ] Inspect the available coordinate operations and datum
    transformations QGIS offers for the relevant CRS pair.
-   [ ] Record which transformation QGIS uses and whether required
    grid-shift resources are available.
-   [ ] Do not choose a transformation solely because it is the first
    option in a dropdown.
-   [ ] Compare known control locations or authoritative reference
    information when available.
-   [ ] Record the expected accuracy and limitations of the
    transformation.
-   [ ] Export/reproject a copy of a layer only when needed; preserve
    the original source.
-   [ ] Verify output CRS metadata after export.
-   [ ] Check whether the transformed geometry appears in the expected
    location and scale.
-   [ ] Document the CRS, datum, transformation, units, and rationale in
    the project methodology.

**Important:** Do not assume one EPSG code or transformation is correct
for all Alaska data. Use the dataset's authoritative metadata,
geographic extent, intended use, and QGIS operation information. NAD27
is a historical datum; transformation accuracy and available grid
resources can vary by location and dataset. If a dataset's CRS is
unknown or incorrectly declared, resolve that separately from
reprojecting it.

### QGIS foundational practice

-   [ ] Load vector and raster layers.
-   [ ] Inspect layer properties and metadata.
-   [ ] Explore attribute tables and field types.
-   [ ] Select features by expression and by location.
-   [ ] Filter records and calculate new fields.
-   [ ] Style points, lines, and polygons.
-   [ ] Label features and manage scale-dependent visibility.
-   [ ] Create a print layout with title, legend, scale bar, north arrow
    where appropriate, data sources, CRS, date, and author.
-   [ ] Save the project and package data carefully.
-   [ ] Export a map as PDF or image and inspect it at presentation
    size.

# 5. Learning area 2 --- Systems engineering and systems thinking

## 5.1 Why this matters

Systems engineering provides the methods to understand how multiple
components interact to produce whole-system outcomes. It is the bridge
between spatial design, ecology, people, operations, information, and
technology.

## 5.2 Core competencies

-   Systems, subsystems, boundaries, context, stakeholders.
-   Inputs, outputs, functions, processes, interfaces, dependencies.
-   Functional and non-functional requirements.
-   Use cases and operational scenarios.
-   Requirements traceability.
-   System architecture and interface diagrams.
-   Feedback loops and causal relationships.
-   Failure modes, effects, and criticality (FMEA/FMECA concepts).
-   Risk registers, assumptions, constraints, and trade studies.
-   Lifecycle thinking, maintainability, resilience, and performance
    measures.
-   Verification vs validation.
-   Change management and decision records.

## 5.3 Learning resources

-   Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge (SEBoK):
    https://sebokwiki.org/
-   NASA Systems Engineering Handbook:
    https://www.nasa.gov/reference/systems-engineering-handbook/
-   INCOSE: https://www.incose.org/
-   System Dynamics Society: https://www.systemdynamics.org/
-   Mermaid diagrams: https://mermaid.js.org/
-   diagrams.net: https://app.diagrams.net/

## 5.4 Applied project: Outdoor Operations Systems Model

**Purpose:** Model an outdoor property's seasonal operation as a system
rather than treating each maintenance activity as isolated work.

Potential scenario: snow operations, landscape transition, irrigation
startup/shutdown, trail maintenance, visitor access, or environmental
monitoring. Choose one bounded process first.

### Analysis checklist

-   [ ] Define the problem/opportunity.
-   [ ] State why the system is being studied.
-   [ ] Define system boundary and what is outside scope.
-   [ ] Identify stakeholders and their needs.
-   [ ] Identify environmental conditions and external drivers.
-   [ ] Identify assets, people, equipment, information, and services.
-   [ ] Map inputs and outputs.
-   [ ] Map the current workflow from trigger to completion.
-   [ ] Identify handoffs, dependencies, bottlenecks, and decision
    points.
-   [ ] Separate documented facts from assumptions.
-   [ ] Define functional requirements.
-   [ ] Define non-functional requirements such as safety, reliability,
    accessibility, maintainability, and usability.
-   [ ] Create a risk register or simplified FMEA.
-   [ ] Identify measures that could indicate system performance.
-   [ ] Propose potential interventions without assuming authority to
    implement them.
-   [ ] Identify what evidence would be needed to validate the
    intervention.

### Deliverables

-   System context diagram.
-   Stakeholder map.
-   Process/workflow map.
-   System architecture or dependency diagram.
-   Requirements table.
-   Risk register / FMEA.
-   Performance indicators.
-   Opportunity and intervention analysis.
-   Short case study.

# 6. Learning area 3 --- Environmental management and ecological stewardship

## 6.1 Why this matters

The objective is to understand the environmental processes that shape a
property and how management decisions affect ecological condition,
resilience, and long-term function. The role is to become a stronger
systems designer who knows how to frame environmental questions and work
responsibly with specialists---not to claim specialist credentials
without the necessary training.

## 6.2 Core competencies

-   Watershed and catchment concepts.
-   Hydrology basics and surface-water movement.
-   Soil properties, compaction, infiltration, erosion, and sediment.
-   Vegetation communities, habitat, and land-cover interpretation.
-   Restoration ecology and reference conditions.
-   Native/invasive species concepts.
-   Wildfire, drought, flood, erosion, and climate-related risk context.
-   Environmental indicators, baselines, monitoring, and adaptive
    management.
-   Field observation and sampling principles.
-   Environmental regulations, permits, and professional boundaries.
-   Uncertainty, data quality, and the limits of remote sensing.

## 6.3 Learning resources

-   Society for Ecological Restoration: https://www.ser.org/
-   EPA Watershed Academy: https://www.epa.gov/watershedacademy
-   USGS: https://www.usgs.gov/
-   USDA Forest Service: https://www.fs.usda.gov/

Investigate the Certified Ecological Restoration Practitioner (CERP)
pathway only as a longer-term possibility. Eligibility depends on
applicable education and experience requirements; an online course alone
does not confer the credential. Verify current criteria directly with
the certifying organization before investing.

## 6.4 Applied project: Environmental Stewardship Assessment

**Purpose:** Develop a defensible framework for understanding
environmental conditions, risks, monitoring needs, and potential
stewardship interventions.

### Assessment checklist

-   [ ] Define geographic and operational scope.
-   [ ] Identify environmental questions and decision context.
-   [ ] Inventory available public, approved, and field-derived
    information.
-   [ ] Describe watershed and drainage context.
-   [ ] Describe land cover and vegetation using appropriate sources.
-   [ ] Identify potential erosion, wildfire, invasive species, drought,
    or flood concerns only where supported by evidence.
-   [ ] Identify sensitive ecological features only from authoritative
    information and with appropriate privacy safeguards.
-   [ ] Establish candidate environmental indicators.
-   [ ] For each indicator, define what it means, how it could be
    measured, measurement frequency, and decision relevance.
-   [ ] Distinguish baseline data from future monitoring data.
-   [ ] Identify who would need to validate the assessment (e.g.,
    ecologist, hydrologist, land manager, agency).
-   [ ] Propose practical monitoring and adaptive-management questions.
-   [ ] Identify potential interventions and tradeoffs.
-   [ ] Define how outcomes could be evaluated over time.

### Example indicator categories

-   Water: turbidity, temperature, flow observations, bank condition,

-   Soil: erosion features, compaction indicators, bare-ground extent,

    regeneration, disturbance.
-   Habitat: structural features or habitat indicators selected with
    ecological expertise.
-   Operations: maintenance frequency, disturbance footprint, resource
    use, or other approved operational measures.

Do not invent thresholds or claim regulatory compliance. Use appropriate
standards and qualified experts where required.

### Deliverables

# 8. Learning area 5 --- Data, software, and AI

## 8.1 Why this matters

Software and data are enabling capabilities. They make environmental and
operational information easier to capture, connect, analyze,
communicate, and act upon. The goal is not to become a full-time
software engineer in a year; it is to become technically fluent enough
to design and prototype useful systems and collaborate effectively with
developers.

## 8.2 Priority order

1.  Python fundamentals and data handling.
2.  SQL and relational data concepts.
3.  Data modeling and SQLite.
4.  APIs and JSON.
5.  Git/GitHub development workflow.
6.  AI-assisted development with Claude Code.
7.  Geospatial Python and GIS automation.
8.  Responsible AI use, evaluation, and human review.
9.  Workflow automation with n8n if a validated use case exists.
10. PostgreSQL/PostGIS and deployment concepts when needed.

## 8.3 Python competencies

-   Variables, strings, numbers, lists, dictionaries.
-   Conditionals, loops, functions, modules.
-   Reading and writing CSV and JSON.
-   Data cleaning and validation.
-   pandas or equivalent tabular-data workflows.
-   Basic visualization.
-   Calling APIs and handling responses.
-   Error handling, logging, and reproducible environments.
-   Introductory geospatial libraries when ready.
-   Writing small scripts that solve a defined problem.

Resource: Kaggle Learn --- https://www.kaggle.com/learn

## 8.4 SQL and database competencies

-   Tables, rows, columns, data types.
-   Primary and foreign keys.
-   One-to-many and many-to-many relationships.
-   Normalization basics.
-   SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY, GROUP BY.
-   JOIN operations.
-   INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE with care.
-   Constraints and data integrity.
-   SQLite for local prototypes.
-   PostgreSQL and PostGIS later if justified.

Resource: SQLBolt --- https://sqlbolt.com/

## 8.5 APIs and integration competencies

-   Understand HTTP requests and responses.
-   Understand endpoints, methods, status codes, and authentication.
-   Read and write JSON.
-   Handle API keys securely.
-   Understand rate limits, data licensing, and terms of use.
-   Build a small, documented API integration.
-   Validate incoming data before storing or using it.

## 8.6 AI competencies

AI is a cross-cutting capability across all five learning areas, not a
separate substitute for domain knowledge.

Develop the ability to: - Use AI for research assistance while verifying
sources. - Ask AI to explain technical concepts and critique a design. -
Use Claude Code to scaffold, inspect, and modify code while reviewing
every meaningful change. - Use AI to assist with data classification and
summarization, with human review. - Understand model limitations,
hallucinations, uncertainty, bias, privacy, and data governance. -
Design human-in-the-loop workflows. - Evaluate AI output against known
examples or ground truth. - Document which parts of a workflow used AI
and how outputs were validated.

Potential later topics: - Embeddings and retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG). - Agents and tool use. - Computer vision for imagery
classification. - AI-supported geospatial workflows. - Workflow
orchestration. - Predictive analytics and digital twins, only where
there is a meaningful use case.

## 8.7 Workflow automation

n8n is optional and should be learned after basic data, API, and
workflow concepts. It does not replace Python or SQL. Do not assume it
replaces any existing automation platform the user may already use;
first identify the actual tool and the job it performs.

Possible workflow: 1. Receive a structured field observation. 2.
Validate required fields. 3. Store the record in a database. 4. Generate
an AI-assisted summary or suggested category. 5. Require human review.
6. Update a dashboard or report. 7. Preserve source record and audit
trail.

**Purpose:** Build a small, responsible prototype that connects

-   Observation

-   [ ] Define the user and decision the tool supports.

# 10. Portfolio architecture and case-study standards

The portfolio should show a progression from spatial understanding to

studies. Three excellent, defensible cases can be stronger than six
superficial ones.

### Case 1 --- Environmental Property Atlas

environmental context, and analytical limitations.

risk analysis, and performance measures.

### Case 3 --- Environmental Stewardship Assessment

Demonstrates environmental literacy, monitoring design, indicator
selection, evidence quality, and stewardship thinking.

### Case 4 --- Outdoor Destination Experience Architecture

Demonstrates service design, guest/employee journeys, physical/digital
touchpoints, and operational integration.

### Case 5 --- Field Intelligence Platform

Demonstrates data modeling, Python, SQL, GIS linkage, AI-assisted
workflows, and human review.

### Case 6 --- Integrated Environmental Property Intelligence System

Demonstrates the ability to connect the other disciplines into one

## 10.2 Required case-study structure

Use this template for every substantial case:

2.  **Problem / opportunity**
3.  **Context and scope**

5.  **Stakeholders and users**

# 11. Certifications and credentials: decision framework

The primary goal is capability and demonstrable work. Certifications are
secondary signals. Do not buy a certification simply because it exists.

## 11.1 Potential pathways to investigate

### GIS

-   Esri training and MOOCs can provide structured learning.
-   Consider an Esri technical certification only if target employers
    repeatedly request it and you have access to the relevant software
    and practice.
-   QGIS proficiency can be demonstrated through projects; there is no
    need to pursue a paid credential simply to prove basic QGIS use.

### Systems engineering

-   INCOSE offers professional development and certification pathways,
    including ASEP.
-   Investigate current eligibility, membership requirements, exam
    scope, costs, and preparation requirements directly with INCOSE.
-   Consider only after foundational systems-engineering study and a
    review of target job descriptions.

### Ecological restoration

-   CERP may be a longer-term pathway.
-   Review current education and experience requirements with the
    Society for Ecological Restoration.
-   Do not assume the one-year curriculum alone makes the user eligible.

### Data and analytics

-   Microsoft Learn provides free learning resources.
-   PL-300 (Power BI Data Analyst) is optional and should be considered
    only if target roles require Power BI and an appropriate Windows or
    cloud environment is available.
-   Python and SQL skills are better demonstrated through reproducible
    projects than generic certificates alone.

### AI

-   Avoid generic paid "AI expert" certificates without employer demand.
-   Demonstrate responsible AI use in actual workflows, including
    evaluation, human oversight, and documentation.

## 11.2 Certification decision checklist

Before paying for any credential: - \[ \] Does it appear repeatedly in
relevant job postings? - \[ \] Is it recognized by employers in the
target sector? - \[ \] Does it assess a capability I need? - \[ \] Do I
meet the eligibility requirements? - \[ \] What is the full cost,
including membership, exam, software, and preparation? - \[ \] Can I
prepare without disrupting the core portfolio? - \[ \] Would a strong
project demonstrate the skill more effectively? - \[ \] Is the
credential worth the time and money compared with another learning
activity?

**Rule:** No paid certification purchase without a written rationale and
evidence of market relevance.

# 13. Real-world learning and workplace case studies

The workplace can provide context for learning, but the curriculum must
respect the employer's authority, privacy, and operational constraints.

## 13.1 Learn before proposing

-   Build trust through reliable work and operational competence.
-   Learn the seasonal rhythms, assets, terminology, and constraints.
-   Ask questions respectfully and at appropriate times.
-   Understand who owns decisions and who has specialist knowledge.
-   Observe information flows, handoffs, and recurring friction.
-   Keep personal learning notes separate from official company records.
-   Do not create unofficial work orders, alter operational records, or
    represent a personal prototype as an approved company system.
-   Seek permission before collecting, photographing, mapping,
    interviewing, or publishing anything related to the property.

## 13.2 Potential observation topics

Only pursue topics relevant to assigned work, authorized access, and
available evidence: - Seasonal landscape transitions. - Snow and winter
operations. - Irrigation and water movement. - Plant health and
maintenance. - Drainage and erosion observations. - Equipment and
material workflows. - Employee handoffs and information needs. - Guest
circulation, wayfinding, and landscape experience. - Environmental
monitoring and stewardship processes.

## 13.3 Field observation template

For each observation, record:

-   Observation ID:
-   Date and time:
-   General location or approved spatial reference:
-   Observer:
-   Evidence type:
-   What was directly observed:
-   Environmental/weather context:
-   Relevant asset or process:
-   Why it may matter:
-   Potential system relationship:
-   Confidence:
-   Questions / unknowns:
-   Follow-up needed:
-   Permission or data restrictions:

Avoid recording personal information or sensitive location details
unless explicitly authorized and necessary.

## 13.4 Workplace-to-portfolio boundary

A public portfolio should use: - Public datasets. - Self-created
hypothetical scenarios. - Generalized and anonymized observations with
permission. - Approved images and maps. - Reconstructed diagrams that do
not reveal confidential processes. - Clearly labeled conceptual designs.

Do not publish: - Internal property maps or security-sensitive
infrastructure. - Guest, member, or employee personal information. -
Proprietary budgets, contracts, vendor details, or operating
procedures. - Water-right or regulatory information in a way that
implies legal interpretation beyond your role. - Unapproved photographs,
drone imagery, or location-sensitive ecological information.

When in doubt, ask for written approval or create a
synthetic/public-data case study instead.

# 16. Definition of done

A learning topic is not "complete" merely because a video was watched.
Use these levels:

**Level 1 --- Familiarity:** Can explain the concept in plain language.

**Level 2 --- Guided application:** Can complete a task with
instructions or assistance.

**Level 3 --- Independent application:** Can complete a representative
task, explain choices, and troubleshoot common problems.

**Level 4 --- Evidence-based communication:** Can document the method,
sources, limitations, and communicate the result to another person.

**Level 5 --- Transfer:** Can apply the capability to a different
problem or context and explain what changes.

For a portfolio-critical skill, aim for Levels 3--4. Level 5 is a
longer-term development goal.

# 19. Final operating principles

1.  **Design the whole, understand the parts.** Always look for
    relationships between ecology, space, people, operations,
    information, and technology.
2.  **Evidence before assertion.** Separate observation, source data,
    analysis, hypothesis, and proposal.
3.  **Field reality matters.** Software and diagrams must reflect real
    constraints, not replace direct understanding.
4.  **Build to learn.** Every course should lead to an exercise,
    artifact, or applied capability.
5.  **Keep the work sustainable.** A year of consistent progress is more
    valuable than an intense month followed by burnout.
6.  **Use AI critically.** Claude Code can accelerate development, but
    the user remains responsible for reasoning, review, validation,
    privacy, and decisions.
7.  **Prefer useful simplicity.** Build the smallest system that
    demonstrates the capability or solves the defined problem.
8.  **Respect professional boundaries.** Do not claim specialist
    authority or credentials that have not been earned.
9.  **Protect trust.** Employer access is a learning opportunity, not
    permission to publish internal information or change company
    processes.
10. **Let evidence shape the career.** Use completed projects and real
    job descriptions to refine the target role, rather than forcing the
    market to match a title.

**End state:** A credible, evidence-based portfolio demonstrating the
ability to understand and design interconnected environmental, spatial,
human, operational, and technological systems---with a clear explanation
of what has been learned, what has been built, what has been tested, and
what professional direction should come next.
